The Prince Who Asked the Eldest Tree

~10 minutes · Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, the Eldest Tree, Big Al, Wilma, Cora the Crow, and every critter in the kingdom

Listen to this story:

Once upon a time, at the end of a long green summer, the kingdom got ready for a voyage.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

The critters knew. The crows knew, which meant everyone knew twice. Wilma had known since the day a young man knocked on her door and asked for oat milk.

The one who did not know was Princess Savannah. Or so everybody thought.

Savannah had a problem of her own that week, and it was a big one.

Every year, she owed the Eldest Tree a story.

That was the promise she made on the Green Island, in the rainforest across the water. There the moss hangs like curtains, and the silver rain falls soft as breathing. The Eldest Tree had listened to every story in that forest for a thousand years. Savannah brought her a new one each year, and each year the tree laughed and called the rain.

This year's story was due at the next full moon. And Savannah did not have one.

"I've told her the flying fish," she said. "And the lanterns. And the sunbread. I have told that tree everything I know."

Big Al hauled the boat down and checked the rope and the sail and the rope again. "Something will come," he said.

"Something always comes," said Wilma.

Spencer carried the water barrels down the dock, two at a time, because he could. Then he stood twisting his hat and said, "Could I come along? For the journey."

Big Al looked at Wilma. Wilma looked at Big Al.

It was the same look they had been exchanging for a good long while now.

"For the journey," said Big Al. "Sure."

They sailed at dawn. Savannah sat in the bow with Luna beside her, black-scaled and enormous. Luna barked tremendous opinions at every wave that came close.

"How about the one with the hedgehog?" Savannah asked her.

Luna groaned.

"Told it. I know. How about the tangled ribbons?"

Luna put her chin on the rail, which is dragon for told it.

Savannah tried nine stories between breakfast and noon, and Luna had heard all nine.

At the tiller, Spencer had his own worry, and it went round and round like the wind in the sail.

He had built her a bench. He had learned her latte. He had written her a letter, and a song. He had drawn a map with a star on every place she loved. He had walked into the fog with a cup in his hand and come back without saying it.

He had run out of things to make.

All he had left were three words, and every time he tried them, they got stuck somewhere behind his teeth.

But they were sailing to the oldest, wisest thing in the whole world.

She has listened to everything, Spencer thought. Surely she has heard this before.

The Green Island came up out of the fog the way it always does, all at once, like a held breath.

They walked in under the moss curtains, and the light went green, and everything hushed. Even Luna. That almost never happens.

At the heart of the forest stood the Eldest Tree, so wide ten Savannahs holding hands could not circle her.

When she saw who had come, she laughed.

Her laugh was like the world's largest rocking chair, slow on a wooden floor. It shook rain out of the leaves a mile away.

"Ohhh," she said, and her voice was wind through a thousand years of leaves. "The story girl. And the dragon. And someone new."

"This is Spencer," said Savannah.

"Mm," said the Eldest Tree, in a tone that could have meant anything.

Savannah took a breath. "I don't have your story yet. May I have one day to find it?"

Leaves stirred far, far overhead.

"I have waited a thousand years," said the tree. "I can wait one day."

So Savannah went walking. She walked the deer paths with Luna crashing happily behind her. She looked for a story under the ferns, and behind the waterfall, and in the hollow of a fallen giant.

And Spencer waited until she was gone. Then he came back alone.

He stood at the roots and felt very small, the way you feel beside something a thousand years old.

"Excuse me," he said. "I need to ask you something."

"Ask," said the tree.

"How do I tell her?"

And then, because the forest was quiet, and because nobody had ever listened like that, Spencer told the Eldest Tree everything.

He told her about the rainy week and the washed-out footbridge. And the frog that sat on his head, and the laugh that bent Savannah in half.

He told her about the kettle that sings when the water is exactly right, and the cup Savannah said tasted like he made it.

He told her about the dragon nest, and the day Luna climbed in and lay down on top of him, and how he did not mind at all.

He told her about the shiny buttons and the fox-styled hair and the mint cologne, and how he washed the whole makeover off in the duck pond. And how Savannah looked at him, dripping, and said, "There you are."

He told her about the winter of sandpaper, and the bench that has never wobbled once.

He told her about the Honest Ink, and the two pages of fancy words that hopped off the paper and wandered all over the village. And the three plain words that stayed. He held up the folded page so she could see. Just say it.

He told her about the song, and how Savannah had hummed every note of it without knowing it was hers.

He told her about the map with too many stars, and the meadow at the edge of the third sheet.

And he told her about the fog. The wrong fork. The lukewarm latte. The tree full of fireflies that lit the whole wood and found him standing in the wrong meadow.

When he was finished, the Eldest Tree was quiet for a long, leafy while.

Then she said:

"Little prince. She already knows."

Spencer's mouth opened.

"She has known since the sandpaper. Go home and say it."

Spencer stood there. He turned that over, and turned it over again.

"That's it?" he said. "That's not very much advice for a thousand years."

And the Eldest Tree laughed.

She laughed like the world's largest rocking chair going over a bump. She laughed so hard her branches shook, and the silver rain came down out of her leaves all at once. It landed right on top of him, until Spencer was soaked to the socks and laughing too.

At moonrise, Savannah came back.

She sat down in the hollow between two great roots, close to the trunk. The way she had the very first time. Luna curled around her. The rain had stopped and every leaf was dripping.

"I found it," she said. "I've got your story."

"Tell me," said the Eldest Tree.

And Spencer, who had come to listen from a polite distance, stopped where he stood in the ferns.

"Once upon a time," said Savannah, "there was a prince who kept showing up."

She told it all.

She told about the night the creek took the footbridge. And a prince who carried every board through the rain without being asked. And how he sat in the water with a frog on his head, and made her laugh until she had to sit down on the bridge.

She told about a cup that finally tasted like somebody made it, instead of like a recipe.

She told about a dragon who sat on a prince, all of her. And a prince who lay under an entire dragon for a whole afternoon, because moving would have been rude.

She told about a prince covered in buttons and bells, jangling, holding up a maypole with both arms. And how he came out of the duck pond plain and damp, and was the best thing on the green.

She told about a bench built by two people all winter, that has never wobbled once.

She told about a village where every sign said something kind and warm and spelled a little wrong. And she said, "I found one more word that night. It was tucked in Luna's collar. It said YOU."

She paused.

"I keep it on my windowsill," she said. "Where the sun can find it. I've kept it a long time."

She told about a song she hummed all the way through. She knew the rhythm because it was the tap-tap-tap of somebody's fingers on the counter, every time he filled her water bottle.

And she told about a foggy night, and a lantern tree full of fireflies. And a prince in the wrong meadow holding a cup gone lukewarm. "I told him to tell me in the morning," she said. "Because it was cold. And because he wasn't ready, and there was no hurry. There has never once been any hurry."

In the ferns, Spencer stood very still.

She had known. She had known the whole time. And she had waited, kindly, without ever once making him feel slow.

The Eldest Tree let out a long sigh, and the whole forest sighed with her.

"Story girl," she said. "That is the best one I have heard in a hundred years."

They sailed home the next morning, and Cora the Crow flew ahead of the boat the whole way. Cora could not possibly keep that to herself, and did not try.

So by the time Savannah and Spencer walked down to the pond at dusk, the kingdom was ready.

The bench had been polished. On the bench sat an iced oat milk lavender latte, still cold.

And in the bushes around the pond, badly hidden, was every critter in the kingdom. Ollie's tail stuck out of one. Thistle's ears stuck out of another. Cora announced, in a whisper you could hear across the water, that this was breaking news.

Big Al and Wilma were not hiding at all. They were standing right there by the bench, arms folded, waiting.

Spencer sat down. Savannah sat down.

He took the folded page out of his pocket and opened it and read the three words that had stayed.

Then he put it away, and he turned, and he looked at her.

And he said them. All three. Out loud, and spelled right.

Savannah smiled the way you smile at the end of a very long, very good walk.

"I know," she said. "Me too."

Then there was a whump. A great deal of black dragon climbed onto the bench and lay straight down across both of them.

"She's sitting on us," said Savannah.

"I know," Spencer wheezed. "It means she loves us."

And the bushes cheered.

Every bush. All at once. Ollie fainted with joy and had to be fanned. Cora shouted the news three times. Rascal waved a scarf he swore was given freely.

The fireflies came out over the water, low and gold.

Wilma said, "Finally."

Big Al said, "He got there."

Somebody started the song, the one from the Music Faire. Every critter on the pond knew the rhythm, because the whole kingdom had been listening for a year without knowing.

The bench held all of them, the way it always has.

And when the song was done, the prince stood up. He took the princess's water bottle, and filled it for the hundred-and-first time that day.

Exactly to the line. Not over.

And they all lived happily ever after.

The Market Where Fish Fly

~8 minutes · Princess Savannah, Luna, Rascal the Raccoon, Pip the Jumping Spider, Otto the Otter

Listen to this story:

Once upon a time, down where the kingdom met the sea, there was a market where the fish flew.

Not by themselves, of course. Fish are not good at flying. But the fishmongers of the Harbor Market had been throwing their catch to one another over the heads of the crowd for a hundred years, singing out as they threw --

"Salmon, salmon, LOW BRIDGE!"

-- and a great silver salmon would sail through the morning air in a high, shining arc, and land -- smack -- in the waiting arms of a fishmonger three stalls away, who never, ever missed.

Princess Savannah came to the Harbor Market on a bright summer morning, and her dragon, Luna, came with her, and they stood at the top of the harbor road and took it all in.

The market was a bright, loud, wonderful crush of everything at once. Flower stalls exploded with color. A cheese man stood beside cheese wheels taller than Savannah. Bread came out of ovens somewhere and made the whole street smell like a hug. Gulls patrolled the rooftops, sharing their opinions. And through it all, over it all, the silver fish flew, arc after arc, to singing and cheers.

But the heart of the market -- the thing everyone loved best -- stood right in the middle of the square.

It was a tall brass machine, polished to a shine, covered in chutes and channels and tiny bells, and it was called the Wish-Whirl.

Beside it, on a three-legged stool, sat a whiskery old otter in a leather apron, with small round spectacles and clever paws. His name was Otto, and he had kept the Wish-Whirl polished and happy for fifty years.

"How does it work?" Savannah asked.

"Simple as anything," said Otto. "Drop a copper button in the top. One of the twelve wish-marbles goes rattling down through the pins -- ping, ping, ping -- ringing the little bells all the way, until it lands in one of the little cups at the bottom. And you get a wish."

"Any wish?" asked Savannah.

"A tiny one," said Otto. "The Wish-Whirl doesn't do grand. No castles, no mountains of gold. It does warm-socks wishes. Found-button wishes. One-more-cookie wishes. The little ones. Which, between you and me, are the best kind anyway."

Savannah dropped in a copper button, watched a sea-green marble dance down through the forest of brass pins -- ping, ping, ping-ping, ping -- and wished her boots would stop pinching. They stopped. It was wonderful.

"One rule," said Otto, tapping the brass. "All twelve marbles home in the machine when the noon bell rings. At noon, the Wish-Whirl sings -- has done every day for fifty years -- and it can't sing if it's missing even one."

Now, all this time, Luna had been watching the flying fish.

She had been watching them with the very great self-control of a very good dragon, only her eyes going back and forth, arc after silver arc. But then a fishmonger reared back and sang out --

"KING SALMON! Big one! LOW BRIDGE!"

-- and up went the most magnificent salmon of the entire morning, higher than any before it, gleaming like a comet.

And Luna's self-control ran out.

She spun to track it -- just to look, just to admire, nothing more -- but Luna was big, and her tail was long, and the square was small.

Her tail caught the Wish-Whirl.

The machine rocked. The bells rang all at once. The brass lid on top popped open like a kettle -- and all twelve wish-marbles leapt out together and went bouncing off into the market in twelve different directions.

Ping. Ping-ping. Ping-ping-ping-ping --

Gone.

For a heartbeat, the square was silent. Even the gulls.

Then, from the far end of the market, a fiddle began to play. Which would have been ordinary, except that the fiddler was not touching it.

Then the drinking fountain by the flower stalls began to pour lemonade.

Then a gull on the bakery awning opened its beak to complain and instead said, with great surprise:

"This gull has found, to her alarm,
she rhymes! But means no harm."

Then a stack of cabbages began, gently and carefully, to juggle itself.

"Ah," said Otto.

"The marbles?" said Savannah.

"The marbles," said Otto. "Wherever a wish-marble lands, a tiny stray wish comes true. Nothing dangerous. Nothing grand. But --" he pulled a brass watch from his apron "-- the noon bell rings in one hour. And the Wish-Whirl can't sing without its marbles."

Luna had made herself as small as a very large dragon can make herself, which is not very. Her ears were flat. Her tail -- the tail in question -- was tucked all the way around her feet, where it could do no more mischief.

Savannah put a hand on Luna's warm side. "We'll get them back," she said. "Every single one. Before the bell."

"We'll help!" said a voice from a barrel -- and out of the barrel popped Rascal the Raccoon, who was never far away when interesting things went bouncing. "Finding things is my whole entire specialty."

"And I'll spot them!" piped a tiny voice from the top of the Wish-Whirl itself -- and there, bright-eyed and bouncing on eight small legs, was Pip the Jumping Spider. "I see everything! I saw where three of them went already! I see everything!"

Luna lifted one wing and made a careful hollow in the fold of it, like a pocket. If her tail had caused the trouble, the rest of her would fix it. Chief of transport. Found marbles ride home in the wing.

"One hour," said Savannah. "Twelve marbles. Follow the strange, find the marble. Let's go."

They followed the fiddle first.

The fiddler stood in the middle of a delighted crowd, his hands in his pockets, while his fiddle sawed merrily away on his shoulder all by itself. Rascal wove between boots and baskets and came up grinning: there under the fiddler's stool sat a glass marble, swirled with gold.

The moment Rascal picked it up, the fiddle finished its tune with a flourish and went quiet.

One marble in Luna's wing. Ping.

The lemonade fountain was next. The marble lay right in the basin, blue as the bay, and Savannah fished it out while the market children got one more cupful each.

Two marbles in Luna's wing. Ping, ping.

The rhyming gull took some doing. Its marble had landed up in its nest on the bakery awning, and the gull was sitting on it as if it had always owned it.

"You'll not take this pretty stone,
this shining thing is mine alone!" announced the gull.

So Pip climbed straight up the awning pole, and while Savannah traded the gull a heel of fresh bread for its treasure ("A fair exchange! A noble trade! The finest bargain ever made!"), Pip rolled the marble gently down the awning into Rascal's waiting paws.

Three marbles. The gull, unrhymed, squawked normally again.

And on it went, all through the market, marble by marble by marble. One in the crate of cabbages, which settled back down into being ordinary cabbages. One under the cheese man's biggest wheel, which had been rolling in slow, happy circles. One by the dock, where a rowboat had been rocking itself like a cradle.

Ping. Ping. Ping. Into the fold of Luna's wing, until --

"Eleven," counted Savannah, breathless. "Eleven. Where's twelve?"

Rascal checked the barrels. Pip checked the awnings. Nothing was juggling. Nothing was rhyming. Nothing anywhere was strange at all, and the clock over the harbor read five minutes to noon.

And that was when Pip -- who saw the details nobody else saw, who noticed the things nobody else noticed -- began to bounce straight up and down in place, all eight legs at once, pointing.

"There! There! THERE!"

Everyone looked.

Tucked snug between two black scales of Luna's own tail -- the very tail that had started everything -- sat the twelfth marble, glinting like a secret.

It had bounced straight back into the tail that set it loose, and ridden along all morning, through the whole adventure.

"It never granted its wish," said Otto, hurrying over. He lifted the marble out with gentle tinker's paws and held it to the light. Inside the glass, something swirled, warm and ready. "Look at that. Still holding it. It's been saving its wish all morning."

"By rights," said Otto, "a saved wish belongs to the crew that brought it home."

Savannah looked around the square -- and it was then, with the marble shining in Otto's paw, that she understood something about the whole strange morning.

"Otto," she said slowly, "the fiddler. When his fiddle played itself -- he was crying a little. Happy crying."

"Playing his grandmother's tune," said Otto, nodding. "The one he could never quite remember. Now he remembers it."

"And the lemonade came on the hottest day of the summer. And the rhyming gull --"

"Made the dockhand laugh," said Otto. "First time all week. Poor fellow's been missing home." His whiskers curled up in a smile. "That's the Wish-Whirl's oldest secret, princess. Wish-marbles never land wrong. Never have. They bounce however they like -- but they land where a little wish is needed."

Luna's ears came up off her head for the first time in an hour. Her tail, it turned out, had not caused a disaster. Her tail had delivered twelve tiny wishes all over the Harbor Market, exactly where they needed to go.

She sat up extremely straight and reclassified the entire morning as intentional.

"Your wish, princess," said Otto, holding out the marble. "Keep it tiny. That's how it likes it."

Savannah took the twelfth marble, warm as a pebble from a sunny windowsill. And she kept it tiny, the way the Wish-Whirl liked.

"I wish," said Savannah, "for one more song before noon. For everybody."

She dropped the marble into the top of the machine.

And the whole market got the wish.

The fishmongers started it, the way they started everything, singing out strong and salty. The fiddler jumped in with his grandmother's tune, playing it himself this time. The flower-sellers knew the words, and the cheese man knew the low part, and the children by the lemonade fountain knew the la-la-las. And the gull on the bakery awning, who could no longer rhyme but had gotten a taste for performing, took the harmony. The whole Harbor Market, every stall and rooftop, sang one song all together while the last marble went dancing down through the forest of brass pins -- ping, ping, ping-ping, ping -- ringing every little bell like it was ringing them goodnight.

The marble landed home. Chime.

And the noon bell rang.

And the Wish-Whirl sang.

It sang with all twelve marbles home, the way it had sung every noon for fifty years -- a sound like a music box the size of a lighthouse, rolling out over the stalls and the docks and the bay. The market went quiet to listen. Even the gulls. Especially the gulls.

Afterward, the fishmonger who had thrown the magnificent salmon came over, wiping his hands on his apron.

"For the dragon," he announced, "with the eventful tail."

And he tossed Luna a silver fish of her very own -- a proper high arc, a low-bridge special. Luna caught it with enormous dignity, and then lost all her dignity immediately in the eating of it, which everyone agreed was the correct order of operations.

They sailed for home at golden hour, full of market bread, with the harbor turning copper behind them and one perfect small song still humming in their ears.

And back in the square, the Wish-Whirl sat content, all twelve marbles home -- eleven that had granted their tiny wishes, and one that had waited all day to grant the best one.

And they all lived happily ever after.

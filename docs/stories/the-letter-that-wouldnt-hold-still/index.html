The Letter That Wouldn't Hold Still

~8 minutes · Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, Wilma, Pip the Jumping Spider, Thistle the Rabbit, Rascal the Raccoon

Listen to this story:

Once upon a time, in a village of painted signs, there lived a prince with a small writing desk by a window.

His name was Spencer. He was kind and funny and strong. He could cook anything. He could fix anything that hummed or sparked. He made a lavender lemonade so good that people went quiet drinking it.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

The kingdom knew because they had watched him. They had watched him carry things without being asked. They had watched him refill Princess Savannah's water bottle, exactly to the line. Every single time. Never over, never under. All day long, for years.

The one who did not know was Savannah.

Spencer had decided that if he could not say it, he would write it. That was a fine plan, except for one thing. Spencer's words came out sideways when he wrote them down. His spelling wandered off. His sentences went on much longer than they meant to, like a walk that forgets to turn around.

So he went to see Wilma, who kept a shelf of very particular bottles.

"I need ink," he said. "Good ink. Helpful ink."

Wilma looked at him the way she looked at everyone, which was the way you look at a book you have already read and loved.

She reached up and took down a small blue bottle. The label said Honest Ink.

"Write plainly," said Wilma.

Spencer said thank you. He did not ask what she meant. He should have asked what she meant.

That night he sat at his little desk by the window and wrote.

He wrote two pages, both sides. He used every big word he knew, and he spelled them all his own way. He wrote MAGNIFISENT and TREMENDUS and FOREVUR. He wrote long, curling sentences that started in one place and arrived somewhere else entirely. He wrote until the candle burned low and the sky turned gray. Then he set down his pen. He fell asleep with his head on his arm, very pleased.

At dawn, the words began to fidget.

One letter shivered. Then a whole word stood up on the page, stretched, and hopped.

Then a dozen hopped.

Then the entire letter lifted off the paper like a flock of sparrows. It poured out the open window in one long, glittering stream.

Spencer woke to an empty page and the sound of the village waking up.

By breakfast, nothing in the village said what it was.

The BAKERY sign said BEAUTIFUL.

The COBBLER's door said CURAGEOUS.

And the Friendly Goose Inn was now, according to its own sign, THE MAGNIFISENT GOOSE.

A hundred more signs had gone the same way. Words clung to shutters and gateposts and the side of the water trough. Nobody could find the bread. Nobody could find their boots. A man stood in the square and turned slowly around. He read lovely things, and found nothing he came for.

That was when Princess Savannah walked into town.

She stopped. She looked up at BEAUTIFUL, where the bakery used to be. She looked at THE MAGNIFISENT GOOSE. She looked at a gatepost that now said TRULY.

And she smiled the enormous smile she saved for puzzles.

"Oh," said Savannah. "Oh, this is the best one in ages."

Luna, her black dragon, sat down in the middle of the square. She announced her opinion loudly, twice, in case the next kingdom had missed it.

"We need hunters," said Savannah. "Three good ones."

She got three good ones.

Pip the Jumping Spider bounced onto her shoulder, because Pip saw the details nobody else saw. Thistle the Rabbit arrived talking and did not stop, because Thistle could outrun anything with feet or without. Rascal the Raccoon came down off a rain barrel. Rascal could climb to any sign in the kingdom, and usually already had.

And then Spencer arrived, rather pale, with his hair still flat on one side from the desk.

"I'll help," he said. "For no reason."

"For no reason," Savannah agreed.

Luna stood up. Luna walked over to Spencer. Luna put her enormous black nose against his sleeve and breathed in, deeply, the way dragons do.

Then she sat down beside him and looked at the sky, and said nothing at all, which for Luna was a remarkable act of friendship.

Luna knew exactly what had happened.

"Rules," said Savannah. "Find a word. Bring it to me. I'll sort them out and put the signs back."

The hunt went all over the kingdom.

Pip found the small ones first. Tiny words had gotten down into the cracks of the old well, where they sat glowing faintly like moss. SO. VERY. ALL. Pip carried them up one at a time, bouncing the whole way, reporting every single one.

Thistle found a fast one. It was ALLWAYS, and it did not want to be caught. It went around the mill once, and Thistle went around after it. It went around twice. It went around three times. On the third time, Thistle sat down in front of it, out of breath, and said, "Please." And ALLWAYS stopped, and let itself be picked up.

Rascal found the high one. FOREVUR had settled on the church weathervane, riding around in the wind, admiring the view. Rascal climbed up and tucked it under one arm. He came down the drainpipe like this was an ordinary Tuesday.

They brought the words to Savannah, and Savannah sorted them.

She laid them out on the steps of the fountain in neat rows. And as the rows grew longer, she began to notice something.

They were all warm words. They were all kind words. There was not one unkind word in the whole pile. There was BRAVE and THANK and STAY and MORNING and SMILE and HOME.

And every single one of them was spelled a little bit wrong.

Savannah picked up MAGNIFISENT and turned it over in her hands.

"Somebody wrote a very nice letter," she said. "And couldn't spell."

Spencer coughed.

He coughed for quite a while, actually. Then he held out her water bottle, filled exactly to the line.

"Thank you," said Savannah, and drank, and went back to sorting.

Sign by sign, she set the village right.

BEAUTIFUL came down and BAKERY went back up, and the smell of bread found everyone again. CURAGEOUS came down and COBBLER went back up, and the man in the square finally got his boots.

At the inn, Savannah stood on the ladder with the sign in her lap and paused.

"I could put THE FRIENDLY GOOSE back," she said. "Or."

"Or," said the innkeeper, who had never in his life seen a morning like this one.

So Savannah fixed the spelling and left the word. The sign said THE MAGNIFICENT GOOSE, and it says so to this day.

When it was done, she carried the leftover words to Wilma in a basket, like apples.

Wilma looked into the basket for a long moment.

"Ah," said Wilma. "Honest Ink."

"Honest Ink?" said Savannah.

"It keeps what is simple and true," said Wilma. "Everything else, it lets go wandering. It is a very good ink. It is not a very polite one."

Beside them, Spencer went the color of a radish, said something about the stove being on, and ran.

He ran all the way home. He took the stairs three at a time. He came around the door to his little desk by the window. There were his two pages. And they were almost completely empty.

Almost.

In the middle of the first page, in his own handwriting, sat three words. Three words out of two whole pages, both sides. Three words, spelled correctly, for once in his entire life.

Just say it.

Spencer sat down on the floor.

The ink had read his letter. And the ink had written him one back.

Savannah never asked whose letter it was. She was busy being delighted. It had been a wonderful puzzle, and the village was full of bread again, and that was plenty.

Wilma took her little blue bottle back and set it on the shelf with the other particular bottles.

"You may borrow it again," she told Spencer, "when you're ready to write three words."

Spencer folded the page and put it in his pocket.

It stayed there.

That evening, Savannah sat on her bed with Luna's great black head in her lap. She scratched the good spot behind her ear. Luna made a sound like a kettle deciding to be happy.

And Savannah found one last word.

It was tucked into Luna's collar, small and warm. Three hunters and a princess and a dragon had missed it all day long.

It said YOU.

Savannah held it up to the lamp and turned it around. It was spelled right. It was a good word. It plainly belonged to somebody.

"Somebody's going to want this back," she said.

So she set it on her windowsill, where the sun could find it in the morning. Then she went to sleep.

And the little word sat there in the dark, waiting, glowing very faintly, like moss in a well.

And they all lived happily ever after.

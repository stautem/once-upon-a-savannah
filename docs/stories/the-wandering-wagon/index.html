The Wandering Wagon

~8 minutes · Princess Savannah, Luna, Big Al, Wilma, Cora the Crow

Listen to this story:

Once upon a time, in a kingdom tucked between green hills, there lived a princess named Savannah who had explored every corner of her kingdom.

Every meadow. Every stream. Every mossy stone worth turning over -- and Savannah had turned over a great many stones.

Her dragon, Luna, had explored it all right beside her. Luna was big and black-scaled and loud, and she liked to finish every adventure by announcing to the whole kingdom that it was finished, whether the kingdom asked or not.

But lately, Savannah had started climbing the farthest hill at the edge of the kingdom -- not to explore it, because she already had, but just to stand at the top and look at the road on the other side.

The road went on and on, over the next hill, and the next, and the next, until it was only a thin ribbon in the distance.

"I wonder where it goes," Savannah said.

Luna barked at the horizon. The horizon did not bark back. Luna found this suspicious.

Then one bright morning, while Savannah stood on the farthest hill, something came up the road.

It was a wagon -- but not an ordinary wagon. It was a snug wooden wagon with a round door and a curved roof, painted the deep green of forests and the warm gold of lamplight. From its front bow, a brass lantern swung gently. And driving the wagon, taking up most of the seat, was someone Savannah knew very well.

"Big Al!" she shouted.

"There she is," Big Al rumbled, in his voice like a friendly mountain.

And beside him, unfolding a map that seemed to have more places on it every time she unfolded it, sat Wilma, elegant as always, smiling as if she had known exactly where Savannah would be standing.

"We've come to take you on a journey," Wilma said. "The wagon comes around once in a great while -- when someone is ready."

"Ready for what?" Savannah asked.

"The Great Loop," said Big Al. "The road to the far wonders. Out past everything you know, and back again."

Luna was already in the wagon.

Savannah laughed and climbed up after her, and that was when she noticed the lantern. It hung from the front bow on a brass hook, and it glowed with a warm golden light -- but there was no flame inside it. No candle. No oil. Just light.

"What makes it glow?" she asked.

"Wonder," said Wilma. "The lantern runs on wonder. And the wagon only rolls while the lantern glows."

"Then we'd better find something wonderful," said Savannah.

"Oh," said Wilma, folding her map with a knowing little smile, "I don't think that will be a problem."

Just then there was a flutter and a thump on the roof, and a glossy black head poked down over the edge.

"Cora the Crow, reporting for the journey," announced Cora, who collected shiny things and gossip in equal amounts. "Someone has to keep the record of everything that happens. It might as well be someone with excellent eyesight and a very loud voice."

"Welcome aboard," said Big Al.

And the lantern glowed. And the wagon rolled.

Over the farthest hill they went, and the road unspooled before them like a pulled ribbon, and Savannah watched her whole kingdom grow small behind them until it fit in the palm of the valley.

Inside, the wagon was the coziest place Savannah had ever been. Bunks folded down from the walls. A little iron kettle steamed on a little iron stove and never quite stopped steaming. And above the lantern's hook, just inside the round door, there was a small wooden shelf.

"What's the shelf for?" Savannah asked.

"You'll see," said Wilma.

The first wonder rose out of the fields on the third day, golden in the afternoon sun.

It was a palace -- but not a palace of stone. Its towers were built of corn. Its walls were built of corn. Along every wall ran great murals made all of kernels -- harvest suns, dancing crows, wagons rolling down golden roads -- ten thousand kernels of gold and cream and deep red, arranged like the world's most patient puzzle.

"A palace of corn," Savannah whispered. "Who builds a palace of corn?"

"Folks who love their fields," said Big Al. "Same reason anybody builds anything."

The corn-keepers welcomed them in and walked them along every mural, and Cora reported each one to the sky as breaking news. And when it was time to go, the oldest corn-keeper pressed something small into Savannah's hand: one golden kernel, smooth and bright as a coin.

"For your shelf," the corn-keeper said.

Savannah set the golden kernel on the little shelf above the lantern.

And the lantern glowed. And the wagon rolled.

The second wonder came slowly, the land folding and wrinkling until the hills turned to stone -- and the stone was striped.

Ribbons of rose and gold and dusty purple ran through every hill and canyon, layer upon layer upon layer, as if a sunset had come down to rest one evening, long ago, and decided to stay.

"The Painted Canyons," said Wilma.

They stopped the wagon and watched the real sunset light up the stone one, so that for a little while there were two sunsets -- one in the sky and one in the earth. Luna stood on a flat rock and roared her approval, and the canyons roared it back to her, echo after echo, which Luna felt was only polite.

At the bottom of a dry creek bed, Savannah found a small striped pebble -- rose and gold and dusty purple, the whole canyon in miniature.

She set it on the shelf, beside the golden kernel.

And the lantern glowed. And the wagon rolled.

The third wonder waited high in the silver mountains, where the air turned cool and the pines stood close: a tall, creaky, crooked old inn with warm windows, called the Stardust Inn.

The floors sloped. The stairs complained. Every room was different and every bed was deep. And at night -- this was the wonder -- stardust drifted down the chimney and settled on the windowsills like the finest snow, glimmering faintly silver in the dark.

"It's the mountains," the innkeeper explained, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world. "We're up so high, the stars shake a little loose."

Savannah fell asleep watching it fall. Luna fell asleep first, of course, snoring in the deepest bed, with stardust settling gently on her nose.

In the morning, the innkeeper swept the windowsill with a tiny silver brush and poured a pinch of stardust into a tiny glass bottle, and gave it to Savannah with a wink.

She set it on the shelf, beside the pebble, beside the kernel.

And the lantern glowed. And the wagon rolled.

But the road home is not all wonders.

Past the mountains, the land flattened out into the Long Gray Flats -- flat gray grass from horizon to horizon, under a flat gray sky. No palaces. No canyons. No inns. Just the road, going on and on, the same in every direction.

And slowly, mile by mile, the lantern began to dim.

The wagon slowed.

The wagon creaked.

The wagon stopped.

"BREAKING NEWS," announced Cora from the roof. "WAGON STRANDED IN THE MIDDLE OF NOWHERE. NOTHING WONDERFUL FOR MILES. THIS REPORTER HAS NEVER SEEN ANYTHING LESS INTERESTING IN HER LIFE."

Savannah looked out at the gray grass and the gray sky. Her heart sank a little. "What do we do? There's no wonder here."

But Wilma did not look worried. Wilma never looked worried. She stepped down from the wagon, smoothed her skirt, and knelt right down in the gray grass at the edge of the road.

"The lantern doesn't run on grand things, dear," she said. "It runs on wonder. And wonder isn't something you find."

She patted the ground beside her.

"It's something you notice."

Savannah knelt down next to her and looked. Really looked.

And the gray grass wasn't gray at all.

There was a beetle climbing a grass stem, and its shell shone green and gold and midnight blue, like the most polished treasure in any palace. There was a smell in the air, soft and cool -- rain coming, from somewhere far away, sweet as anything. Big Al plucked a blade of grass, pressed it between his big thumbs, and blew -- and it whistled, a bright, silly, wonderful sound that startled Cora into falling off the roof.

And Luna discovered her own shadow.

She stared at it. She stalked it. She pounced on it, and it escaped, because shadows always do. She barked at it, and it said nothing, which Luna found suspicious, and she pounced again, and missed again, and this went on for some time.

Savannah laughed until her sides hurt. And when she looked up --

The lantern was blazing.

Not glowing. Blazing -- brighter than it had shone at the corn palace, brighter than the canyons, brighter than the stardust -- pouring warm gold light out over the flats like a small sun.

"There it is," said Big Al softly.

And the lantern glowed. And the wagon rolled.

It rolled faster and lighter than it had rolled the whole journey, because now everything fed the lantern. Ordinary birds on ordinary fences. Puddles that held the whole sky. The moon, following them home the way it does, keeping perfect pace no matter how fast they went. Savannah noticed all of it, and the lantern drank it in, and the wheels fairly sang.

They crested the last hills as the sun came down, and there was the sea, wide and shining. Far off, in a busy harbor, silver fish leapt through the air like birds. Farther still, a green island sat in the fog, crowded with trees so old and tall they seemed to be drinking the clouds.

"Wonders for another journey," said Wilma, and she marked her map, which now had two more places on it than it had that morning.

Then the road bent for home.

The kingdom came up out of the valley to meet them -- every meadow, every stream, every mossy stone, exactly where Savannah had left them. But she looked at it all closely now, the way Wilma had taught her, and she found she was seeing it for the very first time, all over again.

That night, home in her own bed, Savannah set her treasures on the windowsill: the golden kernel, the striped pebble, the tiny bottle of stardust.

And in the dark, they glowed.

Each one glowed softly with the light of the place it came from -- warm gold, sunset rose, silver star-shimmer -- like three little windows into three far places.

Because that is the secret of the Wandering Wagon, and of every good journey:

it comes home with you.

Luna was asleep before the first star came out, sprawled across the foot of the bed, one wing over her nose, dreaming of shadows she would definitely catch next time.

Outside, in the meadow, the resting wagon glowed gently all night long.

Because home, looked at closely, is full of wonder too.

And they all lived happily ever after.

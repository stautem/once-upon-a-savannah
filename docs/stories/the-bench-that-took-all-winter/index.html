The Bench That Took All Winter

~9 minutes · Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, Big Al, Wilma, Mossy the Turtle, Digby the Mole, the Fireflies

Listen to this story:

Once upon a time, on the morning of the first snow, a prince walked up the hill to a woodshop. His hands were in his pockets. His heart was going fast.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

The crows knew. The rabbits knew. The fireflies knew, and fireflies cannot keep anything to themselves, which is why they glow. Even the kettle in Wilma's kitchen seemed to know, and sang about it every time it boiled.

Only Princess Savannah did not know. Spencer had not said it out loud. And saying it out loud was the one brave thing he had not managed yet.

So he had decided to do a brave thing instead.

He was going to build her a bench. With his own two hands.

Now, Spencer's own two hands were wonderful hands. They could stir a sauce and drum a song and mend a lamp that had been broken for years. They could make a lemonade so good that the woodland critters lined up in a neat and tidy row. Woodland critters never do that.

But put a saw in those hands and the wood simply went its own way.

Big Al's woodshop sat at the top of the meadow, warm and dim, smelling of sawdust and woodsmoke. The stove in the corner ticked as it heated. Big Al filled the doorway the way a mountain fills a window.

"I want to build a bench," said Spencer. "For the princess. By myself. Can you teach me?"

Big Al looked at him for a long, quiet while.

Then he took a plain board down from the rack. He tore a square of sandpaper off a roll. He put the board in Spencer's hands and the sandpaper on top of it.

"Sand it," said Big Al.

So Spencer sanded.

He sanded all through December, when the snow came soft and the days were short. He would come up the hill after supper, sit on the stool by the ticking stove, and sand.

Every few days he would carry the board over to Big Al. Big Al would run one enormous thumb along the grain, slow, from one end to the other.

"Nearly," Big Al would say.

He sanded all through January, through the long dark, when the sun went down before anybody was ready. His arms got strong. The board got smooth. He brought it over.

Big Al ran his thumb along it.

"Nearly."

Spencer tried other things too, in between. He tried cutting a second board. The saw wandered off like a puppy after a smell, and the cut came out in a shape no bench has ever needed. He tried making legs from a third board. They came out in three different lengths, which is one more length than a bench can use.

Mossy the Turtle spent the whole winter by that stove. It was the warmest spot in the kingdom, and Mossy had done the math.

"Some things take the time they take," Mossy said.

Spencer sanded.

Sometimes he would notice Savannah's water bottle on the workbench where she had left it. He would carry it to the pump and fill it, exactly to the line. Not above the line. Not below. Exactly to the line, the way she liked it, every single time.

"That," said Mossy, "you are very good at."

The coldest night of the year came at the end of February, and it came with teeth.

Down at the bottom of the meadow lay the pond, frozen silver. At the water's edge stood the old bench. It had been there longer than anyone remembered. The whole kingdom sat on it to watch the winter stars.

That cold night, the frost got into a crack in the weathered wood, and pushed.

In the morning the bench lay in two pieces in the snow.

Savannah came down the hill and stood looking at it with Luna beside her. Luna was a black dragon, big as a hay cart, and she had opinions about everything. She had an opinion about this. She put her nose on the broken bench and made a long low rumble of pure complaint. Then she sat down in the snow and would not move. That is a dragon's way of saying this is not acceptable.

"The Long Night Stars come in three days," said Savannah. "They only come once a winter."

"And there is nowhere to sit and watch them," said Mossy, who had come down to see.

Savannah pushed her sleeves up.

"Then we'll build a new one," she said.

And Spencer, who was standing right there, and whose heart was going very fast again, heard himself say:

"I have one. Nearly finished."

This was not true.

What he had was one very smooth board.

Savannah came up to the woodshop that afternoon with her measuring string over her shoulder and her level under her arm. Luna came behind her, carrying the tools in a canvas sling.

She looked at the workbench.

She saw one board, smooth as a river stone. She saw a second board cut in a shape unknown to carpentry. She saw three legs of three different lengths leaning in a corner, looking embarrassed.

She did not laugh.

"Okay," said Savannah. "Show me what you've got."

And then they built it the way they built everything.

Savannah measured, and marked the wood with a stub of pencil. Spencer cut where she marked, and because she marked it, the saw did not wander once.

Savannah set her level on the frame and watched the little bubble and said, "Here." Spencer drilled where she pointed, and he drilled straight and deep. Drilling was a thing that hummed, and Spencer was good with anything that hummed.

Savannah held the leg steady. Spencer drove the peg, one blow, two blows, three, and the joint pulled up tight and true.

Measure and mark, and cut.

Level and point, and drill.

Hold, and drive the peg.

Luna held boards in her jaws when a board needed holding, and stood on things when a thing needed standing on. Every so often she barked at nothing at all, just to keep everyone's attention where it belonged.

Digby the Mole came up through the snow with news of a flat grey stone. It lay three feet down, and it had been level for two hundred years. It was exactly the right size for a bench to stand on. He and Luna dug it out together by lantern light.

Mossy tested every seat. This took a while. Mossy did not hurry, and Mossy was not sorry.

And in the corner, by the ticking stove, Big Al sat with a mug in his hands and said almost nothing at all.

Late on the second night, the frame stood finished and true on the shop floor. Spencer put down his mallet and looked at it, and felt his face go hot.

"It was supposed to be a gift," he said. "From me. I was going to build it by myself. I had all winter and I never got past the sanding."

Big Al set down his mug.

"I know," he said. "That's why I gave you sandpaper."

Spencer looked up.

"I never once expected you to build it alone," said Big Al. "Nobody builds a bench alone. I didn't build this shop alone. Wilma didn't build that kitchen alone. Every good thing in this kingdom has two sets of hands on it, and most of them have more."

He stood up, and the whole room got smaller.

"You came up that hill every night for a winter. That's the part that's hard to teach."

Then Big Al picked up the one smooth board. It was the board that had been sanded through December and January and the long dark. He ran his thumb along the grain one more time.

He laid it across the frame.

"That's your seat," he said.

And it was. It was the smoothest seat anybody in that kingdom had ever sat on. Mossy tried it and said so. Mossy stayed there quite a long time.

Big Al looked at Spencer, and something in his face moved that was almost a smile.

"Sand it once more in the morning," he said.

On the third night they carried the bench down to the pond.

Spencer took one end. Luna took the other, walking proud and slow, her tail high. Savannah went ahead with a lantern, picking the path through the snow.

They set it down on Digby's flat grey stone. Savannah laid her level across the seat, and everybody leaned in. The little bubble came to rest in the middle.

Then the Long Night Stars came out.

They came out the way they only come once a winter, thick and close and cold and bright. There were so many of them that the frozen pond turned into a second sky. For a moment nobody could tell which way was up.

The whole kingdom took turns on the bench. Digby sat on it. Mossy sat on it, twice. Savannah and Spencer and Big Al all sat on it at once. Then Luna climbed up too and draped herself across every lap she could find, because Luna did not believe a bench could ever be full.

It held all of them. It did not creak.

Spencer sat there with a dragon on his knees and a sky full of stars over him, and he thought: I could say it now. Right now. I could just say it.

Then Savannah bumped her shoulder against his and said, "It's a good bench."

She looked at it a moment longer.

"It's our bench."

And Spencer decided that was enough for tonight.

He reached over, took her water bottle, and filled it from the flask in his coat, exactly to the line.

Then the fireflies came.

Fireflies are supposed to be asleep in February. These ones had heard about the Long Night Stars and the new bench. They had decided that rules about sleeping are for ordinary nights. They rose up off the frozen pond in a slow gold drift and hung in the air all around the bench. And so the kingdom sat in a little warm ring of light, under all that cold shining sky.

One by one everybody went in, until only two were left.

Big Al and Wilma sat down on the bench last, side by side. All around them, the fireflies went out one at a time.

"He's getting there," said Big Al.

Wilma smiled the way she smiled when she had known something for a long while.

"He's been there since the sandpaper," she said.

The bench is still down by the pond. The seat is still the smoothest in the kingdom. The frame is still true. The flat grey stone has not shifted so much as a whisker.

It has never wobbled once.

And they all lived happily ever after.

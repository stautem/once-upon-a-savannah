# Story: The Village of the Friendly Longboats

**Date:** 2026-07-09
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Luna, Big Al, Pepper the Skunk, Greta the Badger
**Reading time:** ~8 minutes

---

Once upon a time, on the brightest edge of summer, a longboat sailed up the coast with four friends aboard.

At the tiller sat Big Al, who had been a boat-captain long ago and had never quite stopped, his beard blowing back in the sea wind like a flag that meant good weather. In the middle sat Princess Savannah, watching the green coast slide by. At the very front stood Luna the dragon -- big, black-scaled, and loud -- chin high, spray on her face, telling the sea where to go.

And tucked snug in a coil of rope sat Pepper the Skunk, who had the finest nose in all the kingdom and had always wanted to smell a proper sea.

"Salt," she reported blissfully, eyes closed. "And cold green water. And fish, and cedar, and somewhere -- far off -- somebody is baking."

"That it does," said Big Al. "And around this point, you'll see the finest thing on it."

The boat rounded the headland, and there was Solvik.

It was a village of painted wooden houses -- red and blue and honey-yellow -- tucked around a bright little bay, with rooftops trimmed and carved like the boats themselves. And the harbor was full of longboats, every one with a carved dragon head rising from its prow.

Luna saw the dragon heads at once.

She stood very tall in the bow and greeted them, politely but firmly, dragon to dragon.

The carved dragons did not answer.

Luna greeted them again, louder.

The carved dragons still did not answer.

Luna narrowed her eyes. She had met suspicious characters -- koi, shadows, waves -- but never anything so rude as this. She kept one eye on the prows from that moment on.

The village met them at the dock, and it was exactly like its houses: big, bright, and warm. The boat-folk of Solvik were big-bearded, big-sweatered, and big-hearted; they knew Big Al of old, and there was a great deal of back-slapping.

They had come for midsummer -- the longest day of the year, when Solvik set long tables down the dock, strung lanterns between the mastheads, and feasted from golden afternoon to golden evening.

The feast was in two days. And the village, Savannah noticed, was in a quiet flap about it. She noticed it most in the square, where two aunties stood shaking their heads over a basket of bread. Perfectly good bread. Golden-ish. Braided-ish.

"It isn't sunbread," said the first auntie sadly.

"It isn't sunbread at all," agreed the second.

"What's sunbread?" asked Savannah.

Both aunties turned, and the worry lifted off their faces the way it does when someone asks about a thing you love.

"Sunbread," said the first auntie, "is midsummer. Golden as the evening. Braided like a rope. Warm spices all through it -- you smell it from your boat before you're past the headland. There's no feast without sunbread."

"But the one who baked it was old Greta," said the second auntie. "And Greta retired across the water, years back now. This year we've tried and tried. The ovens are good. The flour is good. The bread is fine. But it isn't sunbread. And nobody can say what's missing."

Big Al came up behind them, rubbing his beard, which meant he was thinking.

"Greta," he rumbled. "Greta and I sailed together, before she ever took up baking. Thirty years back, maybe more." He looked out across the bay. "I know the way to her cabin."

So the next morning, at dawn, they rowed across the bay and landed on the far shore, where a fern-lined path wound up from the beach into the deep woods.

They had not walked ten minutes before Pepper stopped and lifted her magnificent nose.

"Cinnamon," she said. "A mile off, at least."

The path rose through the green hush, and the cinnamon smell grew warmer and warmer, until they came around a mossy boulder -- and there it was.

A log cabin, snug as a ship's cabin, in a clearing full of morning light. Smoke curled from its stone chimney and smelled like cinnamon and honey and toasted things. And the whole cabin had a feeling about it -- a comfortable, unbragging sort of magic, the kind that doesn't announce itself but simply takes care of things.

They knew it was magic for certain when they reached the door.

Because the door opened just before Big Al could knock.

And inside, the kettle was already singing.

And around the table stood exactly one more chair than they needed, plus a warm spot by the hearth exactly the size of a dragon.

"Well, bless my flour," said a voice like a warm oven door swinging open. "Al."

Greta the Badger was round and gray-whiskered, dusted with flour from ears to apron, with sharp bright eyes and a smile that had been rising for seventy years. She hauled Big Al down into a hug -- which takes doing -- and had the whole crew seated with tea and butter biscuits. Cups were simply there. The honey found you.

They talked of old sails and old storms. And then Savannah came to the point.

"Greta -- the village can't make the sunbread. The feast is tomorrow evening. Could you give us the recipe?"

The cabin went quiet.

And Greta shook her head slowly, with a strange little shine in her eye.

"No, my dear," she said. "I can't."

"Because it's secret?"

"Because I don't have it."

Big Al's eyebrows went up.

"Greta," he rumbled, "you baked that bread for fifty years."

"I did," said Greta. "And in fifty years, I never once baked it alone. Let me tell you what I did when I was young."

And she told them.

"When this village was new," said Greta, "I split the recipe. On purpose. I gave every family in Solvik one piece of it, and one piece only. The honey line went to the Larsens. The kneading song went to the Lunds -- you knead sunbread in time to the song. The secret spice went to old Mrs. Vik. The count of the braid went to the harbor-master's family. And the words you say when it goes in the oven -- ah, those went to the littlest family in the village, so nobody would ever think their piece was small."

"No one bakes sunbread alone. That's not how the bread works. That's not how the village works. All those years in my bakery, I was just the one who gathered everybody in." She smiled, and the smile had fifty midsummers in it. "They haven't lost the recipe, my dears. They've forgotten they are the recipe."

"Then come gather them one more time," said Savannah.

Greta looked around her snug cabin. The kettle sang one hopeful note. The extra chair scooted itself an inch toward the door, helpfully.

"Well," said Greta, untying her apron, "the cabin seems to think so."

They rowed back that very afternoon, with Greta in the bow like a figurehead, and the word ran through Solvik like a happy tide: Greta's come back for midsummer.

The whole village gathered on the dock in the golden evening light. And Greta stood up on a coil of rope and called out the way she had called out for fifty years:

"Who has the honey line?"

A moment. And then a Larsen voice, wondering and proud: "We have the honey line!"

"Who has the kneading song?"

"We have the kneading song!" sang out the Lunds.

"Who has the count of the braid?"

"WE DO!" roared the harbor-master's entire family.

"And who," said Greta, softer now, "has the oven words?"

And the littlest family in the village -- a small mother, a small father, and one very small girl on her father's shoulders -- said, all together: "We do."

Piece by piece, family by family, the recipe came back together, like a braid woven from strands that had been there all along.

There was one hitch. Old Mrs. Vik had the secret spice -- but Mrs. Vik was ninety-two, and what she remembered was that it was "the warm one, in the blue tin, and there were three of them once."

"Cardamom," said half the dock.

"Cinnamon," said the other half.

The debate got loud enough that the gulls left.

And that was when Pepper the Skunk climbed onto a barrel and raised her magnificent nose over the three old blue tins from Greta's bakery shelf.

She smelled the first. "Cardamom. Certainly."

She smelled the second. "Cinnamon. Also certainly."

She smelled the third tin, the one nobody had been able to name for years. The whole dock held its breath.

"Orange peel," said Pepper, "dried in a sea wind. That's your third spice. That's why it smells like the harbor helped."

The dock erupted in cheers, and Greta laughed. "The harbor did help," she said. "It always did. Fifty years, and nobody ever guessed it."

Then the baking began -- the whole village at once, honey lines and kneading songs and counted braids everywhere. And when the great feast ovens needed warming, Greta held up her paw.

"Gentle and even, in the old days." She looked at Luna. "You wouldn't happen to know anyone gentle, would you?"

Luna stepped forward with enormous ceremony. She had spent two days being ignored by wooden dragons, and here at last was a job for a real one. She breathed her softest breath into the great stone ovens -- not fire, not flame, just deep dragon warmth -- and Greta pronounced them the best-warmed ovens in fifty years, and Luna's chest puffed out so far she nearly fell off the dock.

The braids went in. The littlest family said the oven words.

And the smell --

The smell rolled out over Solvik like the sun coming up. Honey and cardamom and cinnamon and orange peel dried in a sea wind, warm and golden, braided through the evening air. Every villager on the dock closed their eyes at once, and every one of them was suddenly six years old again, standing on tiptoe at Greta's counter.

"That's it," whispered the first auntie.

"That's sunbread," whispered the second.

The feast was everything a midsummer feast should be. Long tables ran end to end down the dock, lanterns swung between the mastheads, sunbread sat golden at every arm's reach, and the longest evening of the year never got around to ending, and nobody minded at all.

Big Al and Greta sat side by side at the head of the longest table and traded thirty years of stories, each one bigger than the last, and at least half of them true.

Pepper sat at the head table too, with a little wreath of orange peel around her neck, for she had been named Keeper of the Spice, and she was so proud and so pink about it that she barely touched her fish.

And Greta had one more idea. The village book was brought out, and each family wrote its piece of the recipe onto its own page. One page per family. All of them in one book, but never on one page.

"So it still takes everyone," said Greta. "Forever and always. That's the recipe."

As for Luna: she had eaten seven braids of sunbread, warmed one set of ovens to historic acclaim, and kept a faithful eye on the carved dragon heads for two days. As the lanterns burned low, she crawled under the head table -- among the crumbs, which was no accident -- circled three times, and fell asleep to the sound of Big Al's laughter rumbling through the dock boards.

Out in the harbor, the longboats rocked, and the carved dragon heads on their prows kept watch over the bay.

And if one of them, in the last of the lantern light, slowly and solemnly winked at the sleeping dragon under the table --

well.

No one can prove it.

And they all lived happily ever after.

# Story: Big Al's Only Question

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, Big Al, Wilma, Finnegan the Fox, Thistle the Rabbit, Ollie the Opossum
**Reading time:** ~9 minutes

---

Once upon a time, in a kingdom at the bottom of a green hill, it rained for six days straight.

It was the good kind of rain. The soft kind. The kind that taps the roof like it is telling you a long, gentle story. The kind that does not mind if you fall asleep in the middle of it.

Everyone in the kingdom went cozy. The critters stayed under leaves. The castle windows fogged. Luna the dragon, black-scaled and enormous, lay in front of the fire. She made a sound like a kettle that had opinions.

And up at the castle, Prince Spencer made lavender lemonade.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

He was kind. He was strong. He could lift a barrel under each arm and not even puff. He could cook anything, and he could make a drink so good you closed your eyes on the first sip. He made lavender lemonade, and lavender tea, and lavender lattes with oat milk and ice. And if you did not want lavender, he would simply make you something else and never mention it again.

And a hundred times a day, he refilled Princess Savannah's water bottle.

Not sloppily. Not roughly. Exactly to the line.

Savannah had a little painted line on her bottle, and Spencer filled it to that line every single time. That was how she liked it. And it made him happy to do it, which is a strange and true thing about people who love other people.

Prince Spencer was brave about almost everything.

He was brave about thunder. He was brave about spiders, even the big fuzzy ones. He was brave about dragons, which is impressive, and about Luna specifically, which is more impressive.

There was exactly one thing he was not brave about. It lived right behind his ribs, and it had lived there a long while.

So on the sixth rainy day, Spencer put on his coat and climbed the hill.

---

At the top of the hill stood Big Al's workshop.

The fire in it always burned. The door was always half open. Big Al was burly and warm, and he filled a doorway the way bread fills a kitchen. He had judged dragons and mended kingdoms and fixed at least four hundred chairs.

Spencer stood dripping on the threshold.

"Sir," he said, "I would like a test."

Big Al set down his hammer.

"A test," he said.

"A grand one," said Spencer. "Give me a mountain to climb. Give me a river to cross. Give me a riddle nobody has solved in a thousand years, and I will go and solve it, and then I will come back and I will be worthy of her."

Big Al looked at him for a long time.

Outside, the rain kept telling its long gentle story.

"One question," said Big Al at last. "That's the whole test."

Spencer straightened up. "Ask it."

"What makes her laugh?"

Spencer opened his mouth.

He knew her favorite drink. He knew her second favorite drink. He knew her favorite bend in the creek and her favorite tree and her favorite hour of the evening. He knew that she liked her water bottle filled to the line and not over. He knew what her face did when it was right.

He did not know what made her laugh. Not really. Not on purpose.

"Come back when you know," said Big Al, and picked up his hammer again.

---

Spencer went looking for coaches.

He found Finnegan the Fox at once, because Finnegan finds you.

"Jokes," said Finnegan. "Obviously. I will teach you three."

He taught Spencer three jokes. They were very good jokes.

That evening Spencer told them to Savannah. He got the first one backward. He put the second one's ending on the third. And the third he told perfectly, except he forgot the part in the middle that made it a joke.

Savannah smiled. It was a kind smile. It was the smile you give a friend who is trying hard.

It was not a laugh.

Next, Thistle the Rabbit took over.

"Dancing!" said Thistle. "Fast dancing! Watch my feet! Watch them! Are you watching them?"

Spencer watched them. Thistle's feet went so fast they became a rumor.

Spencer's feet did not do that. Spencer's feet were excellent, sturdy, reliable feet that had never once been described as fast. He danced it anyway, in the great hall, with enormous commitment.

Savannah smiled. Kindly. Warmly.

Not a laugh.

Then Ollie the Opossum called a meeting.

"What you need," said Ollie, "is a finale. Here is the plan. You pretend to faint. And I, at the same moment, play dead beside you. It will be magnificent. It will be theater."

"Is that funny?" asked Spencer.

"It is devastating," said Ollie.

They did it at supper. Spencer fainted grandly and not very convincingly. Ollie lay down beside him and played dead beautifully, which was the trouble, because Ollie then could not stop. He had to be carried home in a hat, still committed. One eye stayed open, to check that everyone had appreciated it.

Savannah laughed a little at that. But it was a polite little laugh, the size of a teaspoon.

Spencer went to bed on the sixth night knowing exactly as much as he had known on the first.

---

On the seventh evening, the creek rose.

It rose brown and fast and busy, and it took the little wooden footbridge at the bottom of the meadow right off its posts. It carried the bridge away downstream. That was a problem, because every evening the critters crossed it for story-circle.

Word came up to the castle by way of a very wet rabbit.

Savannah did not wait for anyone.

She got rope. She got boards. She got the biggest hammer in the kingdom, which was not a small hammer. Then she went out into the rain with her hood up and her jaw set.

Spencer followed, carrying everything.

He carried the boards. He carried the rope. He carried the bucket of nails and the lantern and the hammer and, at one point, a critter who was tired of walking.

Down at the water they worked the way they always worked, which is to say together.

Savannah measured. Spencer lifted.

Savannah leveled. Spencer held.

Savannah pointed, and Spencer hammered exactly where she pointed, and never once asked whether she was sure, because she was.

Luna waded out and stood in the middle of the creek. She let them rest the beam across her back, a living pillar, black scales shining in the rain.

She complained the entire time.

She complained about the water. She complained about the temperature of the water. She complained about how long it was taking, and about a fish that had touched her, and about the general design of creeks. She made a sound like a foghorn with feelings.

She did not move one inch.

Board by board, nail by nail, the bridge came back.

---

It was the very last nail that did it.

Spencer swung, and the hammer came down, and it came down on his thumb.

"OW," said Prince Spencer, in a voice that was not princely at all.

He hopped backward once. He hopped backward twice. On the third hop the bank gave out from under his boot. Prince Spencer sat straight down in the shallow creek, with a splash you could hear in the next meadow.

He came up sputtering and dripping and holding his thumb.

And on top of his head, calm as a Sunday, sat a frog.

The frog had been having an ordinary evening. The frog was reviewing this development.

The frog looked at Savannah.

Savannah looked at the frog.

And Princess Savannah laughed.

Not the teaspoon laugh. Not the kind smile. The real one. The big one. The one that bent her in half and took her breath. The one that made her grab the rail of the new bridge and sit right down on it, because her legs had stopped being interested in the job.

She laughed until the critters started laughing. She laughed until Luna made a noise like a delighted chimney. She got herself almost under control, and then she looked at the frog again, and it started completely over.

And Spencer, sitting in the creek, soaked to the bone, frog and all, began to laugh too.

It was the best sound he had ever been part of.

---

The bridge held.

It held for a very wet fox, and a very fast rabbit, and an opossum who crossed it twice for dramatic effect. It held for everybody. Story-circle happened right there in the meadow, under Luna's spread wing, with the rain tapping above them like applause.

All through the stories, Savannah kept glancing over at Spencer.

And every time she did, she started laughing all over again.

The next morning, Spencer climbed the hill.

Big Al was at his bench. The fire was burning. The door was half open, the way it always was.

"Well?" said Big Al.

"She laughs when I'm just me," said Spencer.

Big Al set down his hammer. He nodded, slow and sure, like a man agreeing with something very old.

"Took me a while to learn that one too," he said.

In the doorway, Wilma looked up from her tea. She caught Al's eye, and Al caught hers, and the two of them shared a look.

It was a short look. It was a very full one.

It was the look of two people who have just realized that the whole kingdom is about to be in on something lovely.

---

That evening the rain finally softened to almost nothing. Spencer walked Savannah home along the meadow, past the new bridge, which was holding beautifully.

Halfway there, she handed him her water bottle without even looking.

He filled it at the spring. Exactly to the line. Not over.

He handed it back, and she took a drink, and she smiled at the rightness of it.

"You still have creek in your hair," she said.

"I know," said Spencer.

"There's a leaf."

"I know."

"It's a big leaf."

"I'm keeping it," said Spencer.

And Savannah laughed again, right there on the path, the real laugh, the big one, the one that bends her in half.

Behind them Luna galumphed through a puddle on purpose. Ahead of them the castle windows glowed gold. Above them the rain went soft and slow and kind, tapping its long gentle story on every roof in the kingdom.

And they all lived happily ever after.

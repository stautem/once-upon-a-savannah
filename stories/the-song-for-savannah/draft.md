# Story: The Song for Savannah

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, Cora the Crow, Clover the Deer, Thistle the Rabbit, Hazel the Owl, the Fireflies
**Reading time:** ~9 minutes

---

Once upon a time, on the eve of the Enchanted Music Faire, a light rain fell on the kingdom. Every instrument in the meadow slept under canvas, waiting for morning.

Out past the orchard stood a low red barn full of drums. It belonged to Prince Spencer. On that particular evening it glowed with lantern light, because he was not sleeping at all.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

The secret had gotten rather large lately. It had turned into a song.

The song was finished. Spencer had built it the way he built everything, slowly and carefully and about nine times over. The rhythm was perfect. The bass line was warm and low and rolled along underneath like a river. There was even a little piano part, four notes, that came back again and again like a door opening. And the words were good. They were the best words he had ever found, and words usually came out of him sideways.

There was only one problem.

Spencer opened his mouth to sing them.

Luna, who had been curled in the straw like a great black coil of happiness, lifted her head. Her ears went up. Her ears went down. She looked at Spencer with enormous love and enormous concern, the way you look at someone falling off a fence.

Spencer sang a second line.

Luna stood, stretched, and walked out of the barn.

"That's fair," said Spencer.

Because here is the truth about Prince Spencer. He could play drums. He could play bass. He could play a little piano. He could fix anything that hummed or sparked, and he made the best lavender lemonade in seven kingdoms.

He could not sing. Not one note. Not one corner of one note.

So he did what he always did when a thing was too big for him alone. He asked for help.

He whistled at the barn door, and the woodland came in out of the rain.

Cora the Crow landed on the biggest drum and shook herself off dramatically, because Cora did everything dramatically. Clover the Deer stepped in soft as a shadow and stood near the wall. Thistle the Rabbit arrived at top speed, skidded, and hit a cymbal. Hazel the Owl came last, settled on a rafter, and blinked down at everyone with her round and patient face.

"I wrote a song," said Spencer. "I can't sing it."

"We know," said Cora.

"Everyone knows," said Thistle.

"The whole forest knows," said Cora. "It's my best story."

Spencer took this well, because he always did. "So will you sing it? One line each?"

And that was how the strangest choir in the kingdom came to rehearse. A barn full of drums, all through a rainy night.

Cora took the loud line, the big shout at the top. She sang it so loud that dust came down off the rafters. Clover took the soft line, the one in the low middle of the song. She sang it so quietly that everyone had to lean in. That turned out to be exactly right. Thistle took the fast part. He sang it much too fast, every single time, and then apologized, and then did it too fast again. And Hazel took the big question in the middle, four small words. She asked it the way an owl asks anything, slow and round and true.

They put it together seven times. It was a mess.

It was a wonderful mess.

Spencer sat behind the drums, with rain on the roof and a barn full of critters singing his song badly. He thought it was the finest thing he had ever heard.

Morning came bright and washed clean, and the Enchanted Music Faire opened its gates.

At the Faire, instruments play themselves if you ask nicely. Fiddles hover and saw away at the air. Horns puff out gold. Bells ring in tidy rows. But nothing, not one string, not one reed, will make a sound until the Great Drum in the center square gives the beat.

The Great Drum had opened every Faire for a hundred years. It stood as tall as a doorway, wrapped in old rope, with a mallet like a tree branch.

At the top of the morning, the drummer swung the mallet and struck it.

*Thud.*

Everyone waited.

*Thud,* said the Great Drum again. *Thud. Thud.*

The damp had gotten into it overnight. Its great skin had gone soft and slack, and a slack drum has no boom in it at all. And without the boom, the Faire could not begin. The fiddles hovered in the air, waiting. The horns waited. The bells stood silent in their rows. A hundred years of tradition, all of it waiting on one drum with a cold in its chest.

Princess Savannah stood in the middle of the square and listened to the whole quiet Faire.

"A Faire can't wait on one drum," she said. "A rhythm has to come from somewhere. So we'll go find one."

"I know drums," said Spencer, arriving with a coil of rope over his shoulder. "I'll come."

Luna came too. She had heard the word *sausages* somewhere near the food stalls, and decided it applied to her.

So they went looking for a rhythm.

They tried the mill wheel first. It turned slow and grand at the edge of the stream, and it went *groan... splash... groan... splash.* Savannah counted along and shook her head. Too slow. A Faire that started on the mill wheel would still be starting at supper.

They tried the blacksmith's hammer. That was better, bright and strong and steady, *clang, clang, clang.* But it was much too loud, and it rattled the fiddles. And then the blacksmith stopped to have his lunch, which no rhythm should ever do.

They tried the woodpeckers in the orchard. There were four of them, and every one had opinions, and no two of them would agree on a tempo. Spencer stood underneath with his eyes closed and his hand in the air, trying to conduct them. The woodpeckers ignored him entirely.

"Mine's good," offered Thistle, and thumped his back foot. *Brrrrrrrrrrp.* It sounded like someone dropping a bag of marbles down a staircase.

"That's a lovely foot," said Savannah kindly. "It's a bit fast."

So they went back to the Great Drum itself. Spencer took hold of the old ropes and pulled. He braced one boot against the frame and hauled with all his strength. The rope creaked, his arms shook, and the knots pulled as tight as they would ever go.

The drummer swung the mallet.

*Thump.*

Better. Not enough. *Thud* had become *thump,* and *thump* is a fine sound, but it is not a *BOOM,* and the Faire needed a *BOOM.*

Savannah sat down on the stone step at the edge of the square to think.

Luna had found the sausages, and had eaten a truly glorious number of them. Now she flopped down in the sunshine beside Savannah, with her chin on her paws. Savannah put a hand on her warm black side without looking, the way she always did.

And Luna, full and happy and warm, fell asleep.

And Luna snored.

Not a small snore. Luna did nothing small. It was a deep, slow, enormous snore, and it came out of her like weather.

*Hhhrrrmmm... hhhrrrmmm... hhhrrrmmm...*

Perfectly even. Perfectly steady. On and on and on, like something that had been keeping time since the beginning of the world.

Savannah sat up straight.

"There it is," she said.

She stood on the step and pointed. "Roll the Great Drum here. Right up beside her. Hurry, before she wakes up."

Six strong hands and one prince rolled the Great Drum across the square and set it down beside the sleeping dragon. They tipped it just so, until its slack old skin was close enough to feel her breathing.

And every time Luna breathed out, the drum breathed with her.

*Boom.* Soft, and huge, and round.

*Hhhrrrmmm... boom. Hhhrrrmmm... boom. Hhhrrrmmm... boom.*

Spencer had his bass in his hands before anyone told him to. He laid his line down over the top of it, low and warm, rolling along underneath like a river.

The fiddles caught it first. The horns caught it next. The bells came in last, ringing in tidy rows.

And the Enchanted Music Faire began on a dragon's snore.

Now, the opening number has to be a song that already fits the beat. And there was exactly one song in the whole kingdom written to that rhythm.

So the critters climbed onto the drum, and they sang Spencer's song.

Cora belted her line so loud that a flock of pigeons left the bell tower. Clover whispered hers, and two thousand people leaned in to hear it, and you could have heard a leaf land. Thistle rushed his part, far too fast, exactly the way he had rushed it all night, and nobody minded even slightly. And Hazel, up on the rim of the drum, asked her big question in the middle. Four small words, slow and round and true. The whole square went quiet around her.

And Savannah sat on the step beside her sleeping dragon, and hummed along.

She hummed every bit of it. She hummed the low part and the turn and the four piano notes that came back like a door opening. She did not stumble once, though she had never heard the song before in her life.

Because she had heard the rhythm before. A hundred times a day, every day, for years.

It was the tap-tap-tap of Spencer's fingers on the kitchen counter while he filled her water bottle. Tap-tap-tap while the water ran. Tap-tap-tap while he watched the level climb, and stopped it exactly at the line, the way she liked it, every single time.

She did not wonder why she knew it. She just hummed.

The Faire ran all day and into the evening. Then the fireflies came out and hung over the meadow like a second sky.

Luna woke for the very last song and blinked at the crowd. Cora told her she had been the star of the whole Faire. Luna accepted this as obvious. She had always suspected as much, about every day.

Spencer packed his drums into the cart by firefly light. Savannah came and leaned on the wheel.

"That first song," she said. "The opening one. That was my favorite thing all day. Who wrote it?"

"A friend," said Spencer.

Which was true.

Then he took her water bottle, and filled it, and stopped it exactly at the line.

And they all lived happily ever after.

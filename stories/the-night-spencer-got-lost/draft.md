# Story: The Night Spencer Got Lost

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, the Fireflies, Ollie the Opossum, Bramble the Bat
**Reading time:** ~9 minutes

---

Once upon a time, on the last warm night of autumn, a prince decided that tonight was the night.

Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one.

The squirrels knew. The bakers knew. The old man who swept the castle steps knew, and he had told the horses, and the horses knew too. Everybody in the kingdom knew Prince Spencer's secret except the one person it was about.

And tonight, Spencer had decided, she was going to hear it.

He made the drink first. Making drinks was what his hands knew how to do when the rest of him was nervous. Lavender from the jar on the shelf. Oat milk, poured slow. Ice, three good scoops. He stirred it, and tasted it, and stirred it again, and got it exactly right.

Then he took the folded page out of his pocket. It had lived there since the morning the Honest Ink gave it back to him. He unfolded it and read it one more time.

*Just say it.*

He folded it back into a square and put it away, where it sat like a warm stone.

"Luna," he said. "You have to stay home."

Luna the dragon lifted her big black head off the rug.

"I know," said Spencer. "I know. But this is between me and Savannah. I have to do it myself. If you come, you will want to be part of it. And you will be loud."

Luna gave him a look.

It was a very particular look. It said that she had opinions. It said she was choosing, for now, not to share them. And it said that later on, everyone would agree she had been right.

Then she put her head back down and closed her eyes.

Spencer picked up the paper cup, and stepped out into the night.

The fog had come in while he was stirring.

It was soft fog, the good kind, the kind that makes the whole world feel like the inside of a blanket. It curled around his boots. It sat in the hedges like sleeping sheep. It made the moon look like a lamp behind a curtain. It made every sound in the wood go quiet and round and far away.

Spencer was not worried. He knew this path. Everyone knew this path. You walked until you came to the fork. At the fork, you looked for the Lantern Tree.

The Lantern Tree was an old hollow beech at the heart of the wood. Every autumn the fireflies gathered inside it and lit it from within. It shone gold through every seam in its old bark. Travelers in the fog could see it from anywhere and know which way was home.

Spencer came to the fork. He looked for the glow.

There was no glow.

There was fog, and more fog, and a little more fog after that.

"Hm," said Spencer.

He picked left.

Left was wrong.

The path went along fine for a while, the way wrong paths do. Then it narrowed. Then it wandered. Then it stopped bothering to be a path at all. It became a long wet meadow that Spencer had never seen in his life.

He stood in the middle of it. The castle was nowhere. The fork was nowhere. His boots were soaked. His hair had gone damp and curly. The ice in the latte had quietly turned into water.

Spencer looked at the cup. The cup did not have any suggestions.

"Well," he said, to the fog. "This is going great."

Now, across the wood, Princess Savannah was already awake, and already outside, and already busy.

The fireflies had come to her window an hour before, in a worried little cloud, blinking all at once.

"Slow down," Savannah had said. "One at a time."

So they blinked it slowly. The Lantern Tree was dark. They had flown to the hollow the way they did every autumn. The doorway was stopped up, and they could not get in. It was foggy. Anyone out walking tonight would have nothing at all to steer by.

Savannah pulled on her boots before they had finished.

Bramble the Bat came to guide her. Bramble knew every path in the wood and did not need to see one to follow it. She flew in soft loops ahead of Savannah, calling back, "Left root. Low branch. Puddle, puddle, big puddle."

Ollie the Opossum came because Ollie always came. He liked to be near the excitement so he could worry about it properly.

"Savannah," said Ollie, halfway there. "Is this the sort of night where one plays dead?"

"Not yet, Ollie."

"I'll keep it ready," said Ollie.

The Lantern Tree stood huge and dark in the middle of the wood. For the first autumn anyone could remember, it did not shine at all.

Savannah climbed. Bramble called out the handholds. "Knot on your right. Good branch. Better branch."

At the top she found the trouble.

The hollow was packed full of leaves. And in the middle of the leaves, curled up like a warm bun, was a very round and very sleepy hedgehog.

He opened one eye. "This is my house," he said, in the voice of someone who has just gotten comfortable.

Savannah did not turn him out.

She sat on the branch in the cold and talked to him instead. She asked him how he had found it. She agreed it was an excellent hollow. She said she knew another one, lower down and out of the wind. It had a dry floor. It had a south-facing door that would catch the morning sun all winter.

The hedgehog was not certain. The hedgehog needed to think. The hedgehog needed to hear about that south-facing door two more times.

Then he went and looked at it, and came back, and said that on reflection it might do.

So they moved him. Leaf by leaf by leaf. Savannah passed the bundles down. Ollie carried them below, staggering under armfuls twice his size, announcing each trip as his last. It took a long, patient, cold hour. The fireflies waited in a nervous ring around Savannah's head, glowing and dimming, glowing and dimming.

The last leaf came out. The hollow was clear.

"All yours," said Savannah.

A thousand fireflies poured in.

And the Lantern Tree lit up.

It lit from the inside, all at once. Gold poured out through every crack and seam in the old bark. The whole tree stood up in the night like a lantern the size of a house. The light spread out across the wood. It laid a gold road over the fog. It touched the hedges, and the hilltops, and the long wet meadow on the wrong side of the fork.

And standing in the middle of that meadow was a damp prince holding a paper cup. He was bright as a boy on a stage.

Savannah saw him first.

She laughed out loud, right there at the top of the tree. Then she cupped her hands and called across the whole shining wood.

"Spencer? What are you doing out here?"

Spencer looked up. The light was in his eyes and the fog was on his shoulders and his heart went off like a drum.

*Just say it,* said the page in his pocket.

He opened his mouth.

"I brought you a latte," said Spencer.

Savannah came down the tree and crossed the meadow and took it. It was lukewarm. The ice was gone. The lavender had gone soft and sweet, and the whole thing was the temperature of nothing in particular.

She drank half of it in one go.

"That," she said, "is exactly right."

They walked home under the Lantern Tree's light. The fireflies went ahead of them in a low gold ribbon. Bramble looped overhead. And Ollie trotted alongside, telling the story of the leaves as though it had happened to him alone.

Spencer tried again.

"Savannah," he said. "There's something I've been meaning to --"

And Ollie, who had been listening with his whole body, got so excited that he fell right over sideways. He landed in the wet grass with his feet in the air.

Everyone stopped. They fanned him. He recovered. He apologized. He said it had been a long night for everyone.

They walked on.

Spencer tried a third time at the top of the castle hill. The light was behind them, and the kingdom lay below.

"Savannah. I really do need to tell you --"

"Whatever it is," said Savannah, "tell me in the morning. It's cold, and I've been up a tree."

And she took his arm.

Spencer thought about that. The arm, the cold, the tree, the gold ribbon of fireflies, the half-empty cup.

He decided that morning was a fine idea.

At the castle door she handed him back the empty cup.

"Thank you for finding me," she said.

"You found me," said Spencer.

"That's what I said," said Savannah, and went inside.

Spencer walked home along the right fork this time, easy as anything, because the whole wood was gold.

Luna was waiting on his doorstep.

She was wide awake. She had, it turned out, been wide awake the entire evening. She had a great deal to say about it, and she said all of it. Loudly. In the doorway. For some time.

Then she followed him in, waited for him to lie down, and sat on him.

She was very heavy, and very warm, and she would not be moved. She stayed there, rumbling, until the prince fell asleep. The folded page was still in his pocket. The morning was still ahead of him.

And they all lived happily ever after.

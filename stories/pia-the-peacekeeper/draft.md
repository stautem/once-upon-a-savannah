# Story: Pia the Peacekeeper

**Date:** 2026-07-09
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Luna, Pia the Peacock, Finnegan the Fox, Thistle the Rabbit, Cora the Crow
**Reading time:** ~8 minutes

---

Once upon a time, at the quiet end of the kingdom, there was a walled garden called the Garden of Stone Lanterns.

It was the most peaceful place anyone knew.

Inside its walls, silver sand was raked into long, curving ripples, like a pond that had decided to hold still. A red arched bridge curved over a real pond, where golden koi drifted like slow sunbeams. Pine trees were clipped into the shapes of clouds. And here and there stood the stone lanterns themselves, old and mossy and gentle, which held a soft glow at dusk the way other stones hold warmth.

And in the Garden of Stone Lanterns lived Pia the Peacock.

Pia was tall and graceful, with a crown of feathers like a tiny tiara, and she had one gift, and it was a great one.

Whenever a quarrel broke out in the garden -- and even in the most peaceful place, quarrels do break out -- Pia would walk calmly into the very middle of it, wait for just the right moment, and fan her tail.

And oh, that tail. It opened like a door into somewhere better: a hundred shimmering eyes of blue and green and gold, every one of them catching the light.

It was simply impossible to stay angry while looking at it. Squabbling sparrows stopped mid-squabble. Grumbling frogs forgot what they were grumbling about. Everyone stopped, and breathed, and remembered to be gentle.

That was how peace was kept in the Garden of Stone Lanterns, and it had worked for as long as anyone could remember.

Until the week of the worst quarrel the garden had ever heard.

Princess Savannah and her dragon, Luna, came to the garden that week for a quiet visit. But when Savannah pushed open the gate, she stopped.

The silver sand was kicked out of its ripples. The koi were hiding under the bridge, every last one. And from across the moss hedge at the back of the garden came shouting.

"You TRAMPLED it!" cried a voice Savannah knew -- Finnegan the Fox. "My moon-viewing moss! Flattened! Ruined! I grew that moss for three years!"

"Well YOU took my lucky pebble!" cried another voice she knew -- Thistle the Rabbit. "My lucky striped pebble, right off my windowsill! Gone! Vanished! And YOU'RE the cleverest thing in this garden, so it was OBVIOUSLY you!"

"Why would I want your pebble?!"

"Why would I want your MOSS?!"

Now, Finnegan and Thistle were neighbors. Their two snug burrows sat on either side of the moss hedge, and until that week, they had been the best of friends -- the kind of friends who talked over the hedge every single morning, rain or shine.

And there, between the shouting and the pond, stood Pia.

Her tail was dragging in the dust.

"Pia," said Savannah gently. "What's happened?"

"I fanned my tail," said Pia, and her voice wobbled like a reflection in the pond. "I fanned it three times. The first time, they didn't stop. The second time, they didn't pause. The third time --" she took a shaky breath "-- they didn't even look up. In all my years, everyone has always looked up." She folded a little smaller. "I think my gift is gone."

Savannah looked at Pia's tail, dusty but still beautiful. She looked at the hedge, where the shouting had settled into the kind of thick, prickly silence that is somehow louder than shouting.

"Your gift isn't gone," said Savannah slowly. "But maybe this quarrel needs something first. Before the tail can work."

"Needs what?" asked Pia.

"I don't know yet," said Savannah. "That's why I'm going to listen."

Luna, who found shouting boring but water fascinating, padded over to the koi pond, arranged herself along the bank like a large black wall, and settled in to supervise the fish. The koi drifted up to look at her. Luna narrowed her eyes at them. She had suspicions about koi. She had suspicions about most things that wobbled.

Savannah went first to Finnegan's side of the hedge.

Finnegan was pacing in front of his moss patch -- and it really was trampled, pressed flat in a wandering line right through the middle, in the very spot where he liked to sit on clear nights and watch the moon rise.

"Three years," he muttered. "You can't rush moss. Everyone thinks you can. You can't."

Savannah didn't argue, and she didn't hurry him. She sat down on a stone and listened.

Finnegan talked. He talked about moss, and about moonrise, and about how quiet the mornings had been this week, and then, when he had run all the way out of jokes, he said the true thing underneath.

"I'd been saving one up, you know," he said quietly. "A joke. My best one all week. I was going to tell it to Thistle over the hedge, the way I always do." He looked at the flattened moss. "When I saw this, I thought... I thought it meant he didn't care. About the moss. About the mornings. About -- any of it."

Savannah nodded, and said nothing, and listened a little longer than was comfortable.

Then she went to Thistle's side of the hedge.

Thistle was hopping in worried circles around his window box, talking before Savannah had even sat down -- about the pebble, and the windowsill, and exactly where it had been, and exactly when he'd seen it last, words tumbling out at Thistle-speed, which is very fast indeed.

Savannah sat, and listened, and waited. And eventually, the way a stream slows when it reaches a pool, Thistle slowed.

"It's just a pebble," he said at last, in a small voice. "I know it's just a pebble. It's lucky because..." He rubbed his nose. "Because Finnegan gave it to me. Found it on one of his clever adventures and gave it to me, ages ago. And when it went missing, it felt like --" his ears drooped "-- like the friendship went missing."

Savannah nodded, and said nothing, and listened a little longer than was comfortable.

Then she stood up, and dusted off her knees, and did some detective work.

She knelt at the edge of the moss patch and looked closely -- the way you look when you really want to see. The trampled line wasn't fox-shaped or rabbit-shaped at all. It was a track of small, light, pressed prints, hopping prints, leading right across the moss toward Thistle's windowsill -- and stopping just below it.

Something small had crossed the moss to reach something shiny.

"Hmm," said Savannah.

And that was exactly when Cora the Crow swooped over the garden wall at full volume.

"BREAKING NEWS!" she announced, landing on the hedge with a flourish. "GARDEN IN TURMOIL! FRIENDSHIP ON THE ROCKS! THIS REPORTER HAS BEEN FOLLOWING THE STORY SINCE --"

Cora stopped.

Everyone was looking at her. More exactly, everyone was looking at her satchel -- the little bag where Cora kept her collection of shiny things, which had come open just a crack.

And glinting through the crack, striped and small and unmistakable, was a lucky pebble.

The garden went very, very quiet.

"Ah," said Cora.

She looked at the pebble, then at the moss patch with its small hopping tracks, and her feathers slowly puffed up with the terrible, dawning realization that she was the breaking news.

To her credit, Cora did not fly away. She drew herself up, cleared her throat, and confessed at full volume, because Cora did everything at full volume.

"IT WAS ME," she announced. "I saw it shining at dawn, and I only meant to BORROW it, because it is FRANKLY the finest pebble in this kingdom, and I hopped across the moss to reach it, and I am now realizing that was YOUR moss, and this was YOUR pebble, and I have caused ALL OF THIS." She bowed so low her beak touched the hedge. "This reporter... deeply... regrets... everything."

She placed the striped pebble gently in Thistle's paws.

And here is the strange thing, the true thing, the thing Savannah understood better than anyone in the garden that day:

the quarrel still didn't end.

The mystery was solved. The pebble was back. And Finnegan and Thistle stood on their two sides of the hedge, stiff and quiet, not quite looking at each other. Because by now, the quarrel had grown bigger than the pebble and the moss. They had shouted. They had said prickly things. And prickly things, once said, stand in the way like a second hedge.

So Savannah stepped into the middle, where Pia usually stood.

"Finnegan," she said, "do you know why that pebble was lucky? Because you gave it to him. Losing it felt like losing you."

Finnegan's head came up.

"And Thistle," she said, "do you know why Finnegan's been so quiet this week? He was saving up his best joke to tell you over the hedge. When the moss got trampled, he thought it meant you didn't care."

Thistle's ears came up.

The two friends looked at each other over the moss hedge -- really looked, for the first time all week.

And that was when Savannah nodded to Pia.

Pia rose. She shook the dust from her feathers. She walked, calm and tall, to the gap in the hedge, and she waited for just the right moment.

And she fanned her tail.

A hundred shimmering eyes of blue and green and gold opened all at once, catching the light of the stone lanterns, which were just beginning their evening glow -- and this time, both friends were looking. The peace came down over the garden like a blanket being smoothed over a bed. Finnegan breathed out. Thistle breathed out. Even Cora breathed out, though she made it sound like an exclusive.

"Thistle," said Finnegan, a little rusty, a little warm, "want to hear a joke? I've been saving it."

It was, in fact, his best one all week. Thistle laughed so hard he fell over backward into the soft grass, and kicked his feet, and had to be helped up, and then laughed again.

The lucky striped pebble went back on Thistle's windowsill -- turned, just slightly, so that it could be seen from both sides of the hedge.

Later, when the garden had gone soft and blue with evening, Pia found Savannah on the red bridge.

"You know," said Pia, "I had forgotten something, and today I remembered it. The tail never made the peace. Not once, in all these years." She looked out over her garden. "It only reminds folks of the peace they already want. Someone still has to find out what's in the way. The listening is the peacekeeping. Today, the peacekeeper was you."

"The tail helped," said Savannah.

"The tail," said Pia, with the smallest, proudest shimmer, "is very good at its job."

They had tea on the red arched bridge, all of them together -- Savannah and Pia, Finnegan and Thistle trading jokes and interrupting each other happily, Cora reporting the evening's events to no one in particular. The silver sand had been raked into fresh ripples, with three new swirls down in the corner: one for a fox, one for a rabbit, and one -- at Cora's polite, full-volume request -- for a crow who had learned about borrowing.

The koi came out from under the bridge at last. They did one slow, respectful circle around Luna, who had fallen asleep on the bank in the shape of a question mark. The koi had discussed it among themselves and decided she was a very large, very quiet koi, and as far as anyone knows, they believe it to this day.

The stone lanterns glowed. The garden breathed. Pia's tail shimmered in the lantern light -- not because anyone was quarreling, but just because it was beautiful.

And they all lived happily ever after.

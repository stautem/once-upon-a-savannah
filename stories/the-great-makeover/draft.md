# Story: The Great Makeover of Prince Spencer

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Characters:** Princess Savannah, Prince Spencer, Luna, Cora the Crow, Finnegan the Fox, Pepper the Skunk, Thistle the Rabbit
**Reading time:** ~8 minutes

---

Once upon a time, on the morning of the Ribbon Festival, the village green woke up early and put on its best colors.

The bakers set out tarts in rows. The fiddlers tuned under the apple tree. In the middle of the green stood the maypole, tall and white and waiting, with a hundred colored ribbons hanging from its top.

Ribbon Festival morning was the coziest morning of the whole year.

And in the hedge at the edge of the green, three friends were whispering.

Now, Prince Spencer had a secret, and the whole kingdom knew it but one. The bakers knew. The fiddlers knew. The ducks on the pond knew, and ducks are not known for paying attention.

And Cora the Crow, Finnegan the Fox, and Pepper the Skunk had decided to help. Whether he liked it or not.

"A prince," said Cora, "cannot say a big thing while looking ordinary."

"Agreed," said Finnegan, who had a comb.

"I have been practicing," said Pepper, very softly. "Six weeks. Wild mint and creek water. It is quite lovely, I think. I hope."

"Then it is settled," said Cora. "Today, we make him magnificent."

Spencer was already on the green, of course. He was always there first. He was carrying benches two at a time and setting them in a nice curve. Then Princess Savannah came down the lane, with Luna the dragon booming along beside her. Spencer took her water bottle without a word. He filled it at the pump and handed it back.

Exactly to the line. Not a drop over, not a drop under.

"Thank you," said Savannah.

"It is my only real talent," said Spencer.

Then three friends walked up in a row. They looked at him the way you look at a room you are about to rearrange.

"Spencer," said Cora. "Come with us."

Finnegan's den had been redecorated for the occasion. There was a stump for a chair. There was a brush, a comb, and a bit of broken mirror propped against a root. It was, Finnegan announced, a salon.

Spencer sat down on the stump, because he was the sort of person who sat down when friends asked him to.

There were three treatments.

The first treatment was Cora's. She had been collecting for weeks, and she brought it all. Brass buttons. A curl of ribbon. A bit of blue glass. A bell from a sleigh. Three coins with holes in them. And a silver spoon, polished until it hurt to look at.

She pinned every single one to him.

"Now you shine," said Cora, stepping back. "Now you are worth an announcement."

The second treatment was Finnegan's. He combed Spencer's hair up and forward, then up some more, and held it there with a dab of pine sap.

"Like a fox in a good mood," said Finnegan proudly.

The third treatment was Pepper's. She came forward carefully, holding a tiny glass bottle in both paws. She carried it the way you carry something you have worked on for six weeks.

"May I?" she asked.

"Please," said Spencer.

She dabbed it behind his ears. One dab. Then, after thinking hard, a second dab.

The whole den filled with wild mint. It was cool and green and wonderful, like the moment you open a window in spring.

"Pepper," said Spencer, honestly amazed, "that is the best thing I have ever smelled."

Pepper's tail went straight up. "Truly?"

"Truly."

"It is also," said Finnegan, sniffing happily, "quite strong."

"They will smell you from the next meadow," said Cora.

"They will smell him from the meadow after that," said Finnegan.

Pepper looked worried. But Spencer stood up and said, "Then the next meadow is lucky." And she glowed for the rest of the day.

Spencer looked into the bit of mirror. A stranger looked back, gleaming and spiked and jingling.

He took one step. He went *tink, jingle, tink.*

He took three steps. He went *tink, jingle, tink, tink, jingle, TINK.*

"You look," said Thistle the Rabbit, who had hopped over to see what the smell was, "like a wind chime that got dressed up."

"I feel like one too," said Spencer. And then he laughed, because he could not do anything about it, and the bell on his shoulder laughed with him.

Down on the green, something had gone wrong.

The wind had come through in the night. Not a mean wind. Just a busy one. It had taken all one hundred maypole ribbons and wound them around and around the pole. Over and under and through. Now they were one enormous knot, the size of a haystack.

Every grown-up in the village had pulled at it. Every pull had made it worse. Now the ribbons were tight, and the pole was leaning, and the fiddlers had stopped tuning, and nobody knew what to do.

"There cannot be a Ribbon Festival," said the baker sadly, "without ribbons."

Then Princess Savannah arrived, and she saw the knot, and her whole face lit up.

Because Savannah loved nothing in this world better than a puzzle.

She walked around the pole once, slowly. She walked around it twice. She walked around it three times, with Luna pacing beside her, muttering dragon opinions at the knot.

"Nobody pull anything," said Savannah. "Not one thread. I need to see it."

She saw it. Blue went under green. Green went over yellow. Yellow went twice around and came back on itself. It was not a mess. It was a pattern, and patterns can be read.

"I can undo this," she said. "But the pole is leaning. If it tips while I work, the whole thing pulls tight again." She looked up. "I need someone strong to hold it. Someone who will not move."

And up the green, *tink, jingle, TINK*, came a prince covered in spoons.

The crowd turned. Somebody dropped a tart.

"I can hold a pole," said Spencer.

He put both arms around the maypole, set his feet, and pulled it straight. Then he held it the way a good post holds a fence.

Savannah climbed onto Luna's back. Up along the pole they went, one careful wingbeat at a time. Luna was loud and demanding about everything in the world except this.

Then Savannah went to work.

"Blue under," she said. Thistle raced around the pole with the blue end in his teeth. "Green over. Yellow all the way around, twice, the long way."

Ribbon by ribbon, the haystack loosened.

And every time Spencer shifted his grip, he jingled.

And crows, as everyone knows, hear very well. They also have no self-control at all about shiny things.

They came from the orchard. They came from the church roof. They came from three counties over. In ten minutes there were crows on his shoulders and crows on his head. One hung off the silver spoon like a bell rope. And Spencer stood perfectly still in the middle of it all, holding the pole, blinking.

"GET YOUR OWN," hollered Cora. "I collected these MYSELF."

"Savannah," Spencer called up, politely, "I am being worn as a perch."

And Savannah, halfway up a dragon with a hundred ribbons in her hands, stopped and stared at all those crows.

Because she had just hit the hard part of the puzzle.

The last thirty ribbons had to come up off the pole all at once, straight up, all together. One hand could not do it. Ten hands could not do it. She needed thirty things that could fly in thirty directions at the same moment.

She was, right now, looking at about a hundred of them.

"Spencer," she said, "hold still."

"That is my other real talent," said Spencer.

Savannah came down and went to him, and she unpinned Cora's shiny bits one at a time. A brass button. The blue glass. A coin with a hole in it. She held each one out to a crow and offered a trade. One shiny thing, for one ribbon end carried straight up.

Crows are bold, loud, dramatic, and very fond of a good deal. Every single one said yes.

Cora organized them, because somebody had to, and because they were, technically, her buttons.

Savannah stood back with her hand in the air.

"One," she said. The crows shuffled. "Two." A hundred wings opened. "THREE."

A hundred crows went up at once.

A hundred ribbons went with them, straight up and out and open. The great knot unfolded into the air like a flower opening in the sun. Red and gold and blue and green. The whole village gasped.

And Prince Spencer had no bells left on him, and nothing shiny anywhere. He was still holding the pole with both arms. He cheered so hard that his combed-up hair gave up and fell flat in the wind.

The ribbons came down straight. Every one.

The fiddlers started. The festival began, an hour late and happier for it.

Spencer had held a leaning pole for a very long time. He was extremely warm, and he smelled powerfully of wild mint. So he did the only sensible thing. He walked into the duck pond up to his shoulders.

The ducks made room. The ducks had known about the secret for years.

He came out dripping. Flat hair. No buttons, no bell, no spoon. Plain as bread and twice as damp.

Savannah was waiting on the bank with two cups of lemonade. She looked at him for a moment, and she smiled.

"There you are," she said.

From the hedge, three friends watched.

"The makeover," said Cora, "was a complete success."

"Total," said Finnegan.

"I thought so too," said Pepper.

So there were ribbons, and there was dancing, and the fiddlers played until the stars came out.

Luna wore the silver spoon on a ribbon around her neck, like a medal. She turned slowly all evening so everyone got a chance to admire it. Everyone did, loudly, because she made sure of it.

Three villagers asked Pepper for her cologne recipe. She said yes to all three, and then she had to sit down.

Spencer refilled Savannah's water bottle at the pump, exactly to the line. And then they danced. One plain damp prince, and one princess who had read a knot like a book. Nobody in the whole kingdom could tell which of them was happier.

And they all lived happily ever after.
